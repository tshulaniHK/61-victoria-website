Our year in four questions

Every year, 61 Victoria takes on a social impact project rooted in the UN Sustainable Development Goals. In 2026 we continued the partnership with Makupula Secondary School started by 59 and 63 Victoria. This portfolio documents what we did, who joined us at our table, and what we learned.

1

Why?

Fee-paying Quintile 4–5 schools have career guidance built in. Many no-fee Quintile 1–3 schools don’t, and university can feel a world away.

2

What?

Three sessions where 61 Victoria housemates mentor Makupula Grade 12s as near-peers, with support from SU staff and guests.

3

How?

Each session links to a UN SDG 4 target, from equal access to tertiary education (4.3) to scholarships (4.b) and skills for work (4.4).

4

Impact

30 learners on campus, three sessions delivered, six guest dinners, and a handover to the next houses in 2027.

SDG 4 · Quality Education

The Project

Pathways Beyond Matric: near-peer mentorship that makes university feel real for Grade 12 learners at Makupula Secondary School, Kayamandi.

1 Why? · SDG 4

Where you go to school shouldn’t decide where you go next.

  • Fee-paying Quintile 4–5 schools have career guidance built in. Many no-fee Quintile 1–3 schools don’t.
  • Makupula is only a few kilometres from our house, yet university can feel a world away.
  • We hold the know-how on applying, funding and campus life. Listen, Live & Learn means sharing it.
2 What? · Our project

Three sessions where 61 Victoria housemates mentor Makupula Grade 12s as near-peers, with support from SU staff and guests.

1Show Q1–3 matriculants the pathways their Q4–5 peers take for granted
2Use near-peer mentorship to make university feel attainable
3Recreate the career guidance that fee-paying schools provide

What we did

Three sessions, all delivered

Each session built on the last: first make university visible, then make it achievable, then help learners present themselves with confidence.

1 Completed

SU Open Day

Session 1 · 18 April 2026

We took 30 top-performing Makupula learners on a guided visit to Stellenbosch University, exposing them to diverse faculties, campus life and career paths. For many it was their first time on a university campus. It made tertiary study feel tangible and within reach.

SDG 4.3 · Equal access to tertiary education
2 Completed

Bridging Session

Session 2 · 2 May 2026

In the Makupula computer lab, we guided learners one-on-one through the university application process: degree requirements, NSFAS and bursary applications, and residence options. Every student left with a concrete roadmap and the confidence to apply.

SDG 4.b · Scholarships & funding
3 Completed

Professional Identity

Session 3 · 24 July 2026

Our final session focused on building a professional identity through a CV and professional identity workshop. We helped learners present their strengths with confidence as they apply for university, bursaries and work.

SDG 4.4 · Skills for employment

Dinner01
27 March 2026
Feminism, Social Justice & Post-Apartheid Transformation (Praat & Paint)
  • Hosted byIn-Aam Kafaar
  • Report byOfentse Ledwaba

Fatima Shabodien

Feminist scholar, activist & development consultant

A South African feminist scholar and activist known for her work on gender justice, democracy and social transformation. Her activism began during apartheid, working with communities affected by racial segregation, and continued after 1994 through feminist organising, academic engagement and policy advocacy, including critiques of how post-apartheid reforms have failed marginalised women.

Move beyond performative activism. Engage in conversations and actions that promote justice and equality within our communities.
Conversation highlights
  • Political apartheid ended in 1994, but deep economic and social inequalities remain, especially for Black communities and women. Democracy alone doesn’t create equality if structural issues aren’t addressed.
  • Intersectionality: Black women often face overlapping challenges of race, gender and economic status. We reflected on how this shows up in higher education.
  • Gender equality can’t be separated from economic transformation, because poverty and inequality disproportionately affect women.
  • We were challenged to think critically about activism and leadership, and about our own role in creating social change.

Questions we’re taking forward

  1. How can South Africa address economic inequality while also advancing gender justice?
  2. How does intersectionality help us understand inequality in universities and communities?
  3. What role should young people and student leaders play in post-apartheid transformation?
Dinner02
3 August 2026
Beyond Access: Exploring Language, Identity & Belonging
  • Hosted byIn-Aam Kafaar
  • Report byLerato Mashedi

Ms Simangele Mashazi

Junior Lecturer & PhD candidate, General Linguistics, Stellenbosch University

A sociolinguist researching multilingualism, linguistic identity and belonging in higher education, and how multilingual students navigate spaces where some languages dominate. She is also a working voice artist in radio, narration, music and theatre, and started out as a presenter and newsreader at MFM 92.6.

Education equality goes beyond access. It means creating spaces where every language, identity, background and life experience is recognised, respected and able to belong.
Conversation highlights
  • Drawing on almost 20 years in Stellenbosch, Ms Mashazi showed that language isn’t just communication. It is bound up with culture, community, place and belonging.
  • We drew our own language portraits, mapping the languages that make up our identities. She reflected on her own isiZulu, Afrikaans and English.
  • She shared how her accent sometimes made her feel perceived differently, something she only noticed after entering new environments.
  • Housemates reflected on how schooling without Afrikaans shaped their sense of separation from parts of Stellenbosch’s culture.
  • Entering university in her twenties, and moving from singer-songwriter and voice artist into academia, showed there is no single pathway into higher education.

Questions we’re taking forward

  1. How much of our identity is carried through the languages we speak?
  2. How can we move beyond equal access to education spaces where every student’s language, identity and culture belongs?

SDG 4: Quality Education (Research, Pathways & the Emotional Cost of Leadership)

Mrs Delecia Davids

Lecturer in Curriculum Studies, Stellenbosch University

Delecia began university in Civil Engineering but switched to Education in her first year, out of curiosity about how people learn. The switch cost her initial funding and meant real sacrifices from her family. At SU since 2020, she researches curriculum innovation and educational technology, including digital escape rooms, and is doing a PhD on how pre-service teachers develop digital agency. She is a Mandela Rhodes Scholar, a Gallup Strengths Coach, a board member of a youth digital-skills non-profit, and speaker curator and licensee for TEDxStellenbosch. In 2022 she joined the Matrics in Antarctica expedition.

Listening doesn’t mean you have to solve someone’s problem. Support often begins with knowing your limits and pointing people to the right help.
Conversation highlights
  • Ethnography and entanglement: her study of how PE teachers use technology became a study of method itself. Researchers are never fully neutral. They are “entangled” with the people and places they study.
  • A learner bringing the researcher a doughnut on her birthday showed how her presence had already changed the space she was observing.
  • We shared our Makupula project. Many learners have the ability and ambition for tertiary study but lack exposure to campuses, applications and support structures.
  • We talked candidly about the emotional burden on student leaders and staff who support students through crises, and the importance of boundaries, self-care and referral.

Key lessons

  1. The power of ethnography as a methodology.
  2. Opening educational pathways.
  3. The emotional cost of leadership.
Dinner04
21 August 2026
Residential Education as a Living-Learning Space: Mentoring First-Generation Students, House Leadership & Belonging
  • Hosted byLerato Mashedi
  • Report byTshulani Khosa

Dr Joy Petersen

First-Year Student Experience Coordinator, Centre for Student Life & Learning · Residence Head, Nerina

Dr Petersen has spent nearly a decade in student development at SU. She has designed and coordinated mentor training for more than 5,000 student mentor leaders and is involved in the Mentor Programme and the SOAR first-generation student programme. She holds a PhD in Higher Education. Her work on first-generation students, student agency and residential education overlaps directly with LLL’s focus on mentorship and shared living.

“It is not about finishing the race… I want you to enjoy this phase of your life that you’re never going to get back again… you can explore, you can discover.”Dr Joy Petersen
Conversation highlights
  • The degree matters, but “the degree isn’t everything.” Much of the real learning happens in co-curricular life: mentoring, project leadership and house committees.
  • SU’s residence system is “a really productive and sophisticated co-curricular space” because it deliberately brings students into contact with people unlike themselves.
  • On leadership: “Whomever I get around the table as a leadership group, I have as much to learn from them as I have to learn from me.” Visible disagreement is healthier than groupthink.
  • Welcome weeks full of activities often crowd out the practical information students actually need, like budgeting, admin and campus resources.
  • We debated whether renaming historically white residences changes their culture, or is largely symbolic without deeper structural change.

Questions we’re taking forward

  1. How can orientation programmes balance welcoming activities with the practical information students need?
  2. Which support structures (strategic advisors, mentorship pairings) best help House Committees through conflict, representation gaps and succession?
  3. When does symbolic transformation become real demographic and cultural change?
Dinner05
24 August 2026
Reconciliation and Legacy
  • Hosted byIn-Aam Kafaar
  • Report byIn-Aam Kafaar

Dr Wilhelm Verwoerd

Senior Researcher & Facilitator, AVReQ, Stellenbosch University

A researcher, social activist and facilitator specialising in historical trauma, forgiveness and reconciliation. The grandson of former Prime Minister H.F. Verwoerd, he has dedicated his career to actively disavowing apartheid ideology. He studied at Stellenbosch, Oxford and the University of Johannesburg, and his work includes the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC).

True success isn’t measured by professional milestones, but by the healing and positive legacy we leave for those who come after us.
Conversation highlights
  • His time as a Rhodes Scholar at Oxford was a turning point that led him to critically question his family’s political legacy.
  • Nearly ten years of peacebuilding in Ireland gave him comparative insight, including facilitating dialogue between former combatants and survivors.
  • He spoke openly about the weight of his name and the practices and boundaries he relies on to manage stress in high-trauma work.
  • He measures his life’s work by what he leaves behind for his grandchild.

Questions we’re taking forward

  1. How can community leaders apply Northern Ireland’s peacebuilding frameworks in South Africa?
  2. How do we measure the long-term impact of our everyday actions on the next generation?
  3. What personal alienation can come with putting justice and restitution above family loyalty?

Quality Education: Registration, NSFAS & Clear Communication with New Students
  • Hosted byOfentse Ledwaba
  • Report bySikhanyiso Nkosi
  • Sponsored bySU Students’ Representative Council

Dr Ronel Retief

Registrar, Stellenbosch University

A member of SU’s Executive Management, Dr Retief oversees academic administration, governance, student records, policy and compliance. In 2017 she became the first woman to serve as SU’s Registrar, having previously been Deputy Registrar at the Tygerberg Campus. She has contributed across the sector to student selection, placement testing and student support.

“Failure is only failure if you don’t learn from it.”Dr Ronel Retief
Conversation highlights
  • Student registration: its growing challenges, and what clearer communication from SU to new applicants could look like to avoid the annual “January Misery”.
  • The NSFAS bottleneck, and the harder question beneath it: how could everyone gain entry without putting the university’s financial sustainability at risk?
  • Her time in Utrecht, her love of Afrikaans literature, and her journey to becoming Registrar, all while being a boy mom and a wife.
  • The best part of the job: watching students graduate and hooding each one. (Class of March/April 2027, keep an eye on your Registrar. She has something planned, courtesy of 61 Victoria.)
  • And yes, we taught the Registrar the art of “clocking it”.

In her words

  1. On failure: “Failure is only failure if you don’t learn from it.”
  2. On legacy: she hopes the SU community remembers her as someone who was authentic and who cared.
Voices from 61 Victoria

Our Reflections

What worked, what was hard, what we would do differently, and what this project meant to each of us.

5 What we learned

As a house

What worked

Meeting learners at their school and on our campus made university feel real.

The challenge

Fitting around the school timetable and keeping momentum between visits.

Next time

Use before-and-after surveys to measure change, and start before application deadlines.

Inspiring others

Any house can adopt a school: open your campus, share what you know, and return.

Housemate reflections

In our own words

Each of us came to this project with a different background. These are our honest reflections on working alongside the learners of Makupula Secondary School.

SN
Sikhanyiso Nkosi
61 Victoria, 2026

The Makupula Secondary School project has been a meaningful and eye-opening experience. Through both the Stellenbosch University Open Day and our visit to Makupula Secondary School, I gained a deeper understanding of how important access to information, exposure and guidance is for learners preparing for university. At the Open Day, I saw how powerful exposure can be. For many learners it was their first time on a university campus, which helped make higher education feel more real and attainable. I realised how unfamiliar and intimidating these spaces can feel, and how much confidence can grow simply through guided exposure and open conversations. At Makupula, I learned the value of patience and clear communication in mentorship. Many learners are capable but lack structured support around applications, funding and study options, and this experience showed me how important it is to create a space where learners feel supported and comfortable asking questions.

TK
Tshulani Khosa
61 Victoria, 2026

Working on the Makupula Secondary School project has been an enriching experience that highlighted for me how uneven access to guidance and information can shape learners’ educational journeys. Being part of the SU Open Day allowed me to see how powerful it is for learners to step into a university setting. It shifts higher education from something abstract into something tangible, while also revealing how uncertain many students feel when encountering such environments for the first time. Our follow-up visit to Makupula Secondary School reinforced this. Engaging directly with learners showed me that many have strong aspirations but often lack clarity on the practical steps needed to pursue them, particularly around applications, funding and available programmes. Overall, the project made me more aware of how meaningful consistent engagement and accessible guidance can be in helping learners understand their options and feel more confident about their futures after matric.

IK
In-Aam Kafaar
61 Victoria, 2026

Working with Makupula Secondary School, and continuing the relationship established by previous LLL houses with the same SDG, has shown the importance of sustainability in any project, but particularly in those which contribute to the shaping of young minds and their access to fair and reliable mentorship. Our sessions thus far have shown how important it is to share resources and lived experience with the future of our country.

LM
Lerato Mashedi
61 Victoria, 2026

Reflecting on the first session during the Stellenbosch University Open Day, aside from the weather, I really enjoyed the experience, especially working with the Grade 12 learners, who were respectful and eager to engage. Both they and I were initially a bit nervous, which is understandable in a new environment. The second session, where we visited the school with my housemates, felt even more impactful, as many learners seemed more open to the idea of pursuing further education after Grade 12 and more comfortable discussing their future plans and possibilities. One of my biggest takeaways from this house project is how important exposure and access to information can be. I also attended a public school with limited resources, and I often feel that an opportunity like this during my matric year would have helped me transition into university life more confidently by knowing what to expect. Even for those who may not choose the university route, it is important that they now know it is an available option, because sometimes opportunities feel limited simply due to lack of exposure, and learning about different paths can broaden horizons and help people imagine bigger futures. I was truly happy to be part of this project and grateful for the opportunity to contribute in a meaningful way.

Collaboration & support

Our Partners

None of this work happens alone. These partners’ expertise, resources and belief in the mission shaped every session we delivered.

Community partner

Makupula Secondary School, Kayamandi

Our primary community partner. School leadership, teachers and Grade 12 learners are central to every session we deliver. Their openness and enthusiasm have made this project what it is.

Stellenbosch University

Centre for the Advancement of Social Impact and Transformation (CASIT)

The Centre advances, supports and co-ordinates Stellenbosch University’s institutional engagement in social impact and transformation, and supports the social impact work of LLL houses.

Programme

Listen, Live & Learn (LLL)

The LLL programme provides oversight, institutional continuity and a community of practice. It is why this project has passed from 59 and 63 Victoria to us, and will pass to the next houses in 2027.

Non-profit

The UniDreams Foundation

A youth-led non-profit bridging the gap between ambition and access for students from underserved communities. It provides mentorship, university application support and resources to help young people reach their full academic and personal potential.
